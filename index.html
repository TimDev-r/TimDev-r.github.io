03 Try the work yourself

Three real problems from my work, running live in your browser. No background needed — each one explains itself as you play with it.

Finding the few seconds that matter in hours of footage

motion signatures motion-groundtruth-tools

The problem: a ten-hour recording is almost entirely nothing happening, and someone has to find the moments that aren't.

The idea: squeeze every frame down to a single line of text showing how far away anything moved. Hours of video become a page you can skim. Below, the strip is that line — left is close to the camera, right is far away. Flip the switch to compare it against a plain motion detector, and tap or drag the person to see it react.

3.0 m
person is here
0 mclose2 m4 m6 m8 mfar

  • - nothing changed at this distance
  • x a little changed
  • + a lot changed
  • brighter = more of the picture changed at that distance

One person lights up a whole span of distances, not a single mark — here, everything from about 3 m out to the wall at 6 m. Only the near end is the person. The rest is the floor and wall they stopped hiding, which changed just as much as they did.

The marker underneath shows where the person really is, and that is the honest limitation: the strip cannot tell you which end is a person. It reports that pixels at these distances changed, nothing more. Reading a bright mark at 6 m as "somebody at 6 m" is the mistake this makes easy — and knowing it is the difference between trusting the data and chasing a phantom.

The technical version

Depth is sampled from the current frame, so pixels the subject has just vacated report the background distance rather than the subject's. The signature therefore carries a disocclusion trail — inherent to differencing against a single depth map, not a defect. Because the background here is a floor receding to a wall, that trail is a span rather than a second spike, and it thins with distance as perspective compresses more metres into each row of pixels. It's pinned down by a test in the repository so it can't silently change.

Counting people without counting anyone twice

cross-camera matching realsense-multicam-detection

The problem: two cameras watch the same room. One person walks in, and both cameras see them. Is that one person, or two?

The idea: don't compare what the cameras see — compare where they think the person is standing. Two sightings of the same spot in the room must be the same person. The catch: each camera measures from itself, so the same person gets described two different ways — and something has to reconcile them. Flip the switch, and tap or drag to move the person around.

Switch to Before and read the two reports. Camera 1 says the person is to its right; camera 2 says the same person is to its left. Both are correct — they are standing in different places. But the matcher compares those two descriptions without asking where either camera was standing, which is like comparing "third door on the left" from opposite ends of a corridor. The dotted arrow shows where camera 2's numbers land once you forget its position: an empty spot on the floor. Nobody is there, and the system counts a second person anyway.

Lining the cameras up is the step that makes a multi-camera setup work, and it's the easiest one to underestimate. Skip it and nothing crashes — the system keeps running and the numbers still look reasonable. That's the kind of failure you only catch by measuring, which is why it's worth showing rather than describing.

Alignment isn't the only way to break it. Turn sensor error up with the cameras perfectly lined up and watch the verdict flicker: the same person is counted once, then twice, then once again, purely because the depth readings wobble either side of the 10 cm cut-off. Any fixed threshold has that edge, and knowing where it sits for your hardware is part of the job.

The technical version

depth_matching.py compares each camera's own camera-space points without transforming them through the stored extrinsics, so the separation can never fall below the baseline and the 10 cm match threshold effectively just tests the baseline. The calibration step writes valid R and T to calibration_data.npz; applying them is the missing step. Checking the box does exactly that, collapsing the gap to sensor noise. Carried as a known limitation in the repository's README.

Telling this year’s salary table from last year’s

version-scoped retrieval salary_RAG

The problem: Austria’s IT collective agreement is renegotiated every year, and the 2025 and 2026 documents are about 95% identical — same structure, same wording, the salary table on the same page. Only the numbers move. Ask for “the ST1 minimum salary” and a search built on meaning returns the wrong year confidently, because by meaning the two paragraphs are the same paragraph.

The fix is not a better model. Every passage carries the dates it is in force, and the wrong year is removed from the running before anything is ranked, rather than filtered out of the results afterwards — which is the same mechanism a company search needs to keep one team from reading another’s documents. It is worth 16 points of accuracy on the first result, and nothing at all after the third.

AT-KV Assistant recorded replay · real answers, citations and latencies

Watch the second question. It is the same sentence as the first — only the year in force changed, and with it the figure, the source document and the page. Every answer carries a reference you can check against the published PDF, which is the whole point: a salary figure without a paragraph number is a rumour. The third question asks what to do rather than what the documents say, and is refused — five out of five advice questions refused, with none of the twenty-five factual ones refused by mistake. Full write-up and numbers →

The technical version

Each passage carries valid_from and valid_to as an interval, not a year, so an unchanged paragraph is not duplicated once per edition. The filter is applied through a FAISS IDSelector, which restricts the scan rather than masking results afterwards — the distinction matters: on an adversarial case, post-filtering returned 0 of 5 requested results while pre-filtering returned all four that existed. Post-filtering does not rank worse, it silently returns nothing.

The salary table is the hard part. extract_text() flattens it to Einstiegsstufe 2 236 2 547 3 267 4061 5 301 — the thousands separator is a space, so five values arrive as nine tokens, and the spacing is not even consistent within a row. One row is ragged, so reading numbers in order shifts every value one column left. Lattice detection recovers the real grid from the PDF's cell rectangles, and each cell is then rewritten as a sentence, which turns a geometry problem into a text problem.

German compounds are split against a vocabulary drawn from the corpus itself, so a query for Gehalt reaches Mindestgrundgehalt; without it plain BM25 scores that pair at exactly zero. Worth +0.08 at rank 3. The largest single win, though, was a bug: the parser was discarding the paragraph titles the source publishes — § 3 Normalarbeitszeit against § 4 Andere Verteilung der Normalarbeitszeit — which is precisely what separates four near-identical provisions that otherwise score within 0.007 of each other. Restoring one line of parser moved retrieval@5 from 0.88 to 0.92 and made the cross-encoder reranker, at 1.4 s per query, unnecessary.
